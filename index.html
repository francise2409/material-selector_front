Reading the numbers → predicting polymer behaviour

The three values shown when you click an element are the starting point of first-principles prediction.

Atomic mass (u)

average isotopic mass

Sets density, and is a proxy for electron count: larger/heavier atoms are more polarisable → stronger London / Van der Waals dispersion between chains → higher Tg/Tm, but easily swollen by non-polar solvents.

Electronegativity (χ)

pull on shared electrons

The difference Δχ across a bond sets its polarity → dipole forces → “like dissolves like” (oil vs water resistance). High-χ fluorine also builds the electron-rich C–F sheath (shielding).

1st ionization energy

kJ/mol to remove outer e⁻

How tightly valence electrons are held → resistance to oxidation and general reactivity. High-IE elements (F, O, N) form stable, hard-to-attack bonds; tracks the χ trend.

Bond polarity from Δχ (electronegativity difference)

Non-polar chains (C–C, C–H) resist water but swell in oils; polar bonds (C–O, C–F, C≡N) give oil/chemical resistance but can be reached by polar solvents. Very large Δχ (salts) is ionic — rare in polymer backbones.

Orbitals & bonding — why shape decides reactivity

Electrons occupy shaped orbitals; the way orbitals overlap decides bond type. Head-on overlap = σ (strong, along the bond axis); side-on overlap of p-orbitals = π (weaker, electron density exposed above/below). This is why the π part of a C=C is the site attacked by ozone/UV — it sticks out and is easy to reach.

Aufbau filling order (fill lowest-energy first — the diagonal rule):

Shell capacity: s 2 · p 6 · d 10 · f 14. Outer-shell (valence) electrons + electronegativity set bonding and polarity.

Electron configuration → bond strength (guideline)

Atoms bond to reach a stable closed shell (noble-gas configuration). How easily that is reached, and how well the orbitals overlap, together set bond polarity and bond strength — the root of thermal/chemical stability.

Worked example — why H–F is stronger than H–Cl

  • F = [He]2s²2p⁵ → needs +1 e⁻ to reach the very stable 2s²2p⁶ (neon). It is small with the highest electronegativity (χ = 3.98); the H 1s–F 2p overlap is short and tight → a very strong, short bond.
  • Cl = [Ne]3s²3p⁵ → also needs +1 e⁻, reaching 3s²3p⁶ (argon). But its valence shell is n = 3: larger, more shielded, more diffuse → poorer overlap with the small H 1s, longer bond, lower χ (3.16) → a weaker bond.
CORRECTION. Your note said Cl “needs to reach 3d¹⁰”. That is not correct: Cl completes its octet at 3s²3p⁶ (argon) by gaining just ONE electron — exactly like F. The empty 3d subshell is higher in energy and plays no role in Cl’s normal octet. Your other intuition is right, though: Cl’s valence electrons sit in the larger, more diffuse n = 3 shell, farther from and more shielded from the nucleus → poorer orbital overlap → weaker bond.

USEFUL PARADOX. A stronger H–X bond makes a weaker acid: HF is a weak acid (the bond is hard to break), while HCl/HBr/HI are strong acids. Handy when reasoning about acid attack on seals — “strong bond” (durable material) and “strong acid” (aggressive fluid) are opposite ideas.

Apply to polymers

  • C–F (≈ 485 kJ/mol) is very strong, and F’s small size + high χ builds the electron-rich sheath → fluoropolymer inertness, high service temperature, low friction. Going down the group, C–Cl (≈ 327) is weaker → PVC is less thermally stable and can dehydrochlorinate (release HCl) when overheated.
  • General rule: a bond that completes a stable shell AND overlaps well resists scission → high activation energy → higher service temperature and chemical resistance.
CAVEAT. “Fluorine always makes the strongest bond” is not universal — the F–F single bond is anomalously weak (~157 kJ/mol) because the two small fluorines’ lone pairs repel. It is C–F and H–F that are exceptionally strong. Bond strength always depends on both partners, not one atom alone.

Electron acceptor = OXIDISER (not reducer)

A nearly-full shell (F [He]2s²2p⁵, one short of 2s²2p⁶) strongly ATTRACTS an electron (high χ, high EA). Taking electrons, it oxidises its partner and is itself reduced → it is an oxidising agent. The opposite — low IE, gives up an electron (Na 3s¹) — is a reducing agent. In a covalent bond the more electronegative partner just pulls the shared pair toward itself (δ−), making the dipole.

Common mix-up: an electron-hungry atom like F is an oxidiser, NOT a reducer. Reducers are electron donors (metals, low IE).
Nuance: F is the most electronegative & strongest oxidiser, yet its EA (≈328 kJ/mol) is slightly LOWER than Cl’s (≈349) — its compact 2p shell crowds the incoming electron. χ (and the weak F–F bond) drive F’s reactivity, not EA alone.

Why polar / non-polar

  • Bond: Δχ between the two atoms (≈0 → non-polar; C–F, C≡N, C–O → polar).
  • Molecule/polymer: net polarity = vector sum of bond dipoles — symmetry can CANCEL polar bonds (CO₂, CCl₄; PTFE’s symmetric –CF₂– → effectively non-polar, low-energy surface).
  • So: non-polar → swells in oil, resists water; polar → resists oil, reached by polar solvents.

Quantifying polarity — the solubility parameter δ (the number to look up)

Polarity of a whole material is not a periodic-table value (that is per-element χ). Look up the Hildebrand solubility parameter δ (MPa½), or its Hansen split (δd dispersion / δp polar / δh hydrogen-bond), or the dielectric constant ε. Why UV-susceptible / UV-resistant

  • Needs BOTH: (1) a chromophore that ABSORBS UV (C=C, C=O, aromatic, N=N, allylic/tertiary C–H) AND (2) photon energy > bond energy.
  • UV photons ≈ 300–400 kJ/mol (300–400 nm) → bonds weaker than that AND absorbing will break: C–C (346), C–Cl (327) vulnerable; C–F (485) survives.
  • UV-resistant = saturated, shielded, high-BDE, no chromophore (PTFE, silicone, EPDM) + carbon black / UV stabilisers. UV-weak = diene C=C (NBR), azo dyes, long-exposed aromatics.

Why flammable / flame-resistant

  • Burns easily: high C–H (hydrogen) content feeds the radical chain + high heat; no char; low decomposition T. PE/PP → LOI ≈ 17–18 (air is 21% O₂).
  • Halogen flame-retardance: Cl/Br/F scavenge H•/OH• combustion radicals → PVC (LOI ≈ 45), PTFE (LOI ≈ 95) self-extinguish.
  • Char flame-retardance: aromatic backbones (PEEK, PPS, polyimide) form protective char; high BDE raises decomposition T.
  • Parameter: Limiting Oxygen Index (LOI) — min %O₂ to keep burning; LOI < 21 burns in air. Also UL 94 (V-0/V-1/HB).

Worked case — neoprene (CR): why the pendant decides everything

Backbone –CH₂–CCl=CH–CH₂– with a pendant Cl on the double bond. That one chlorine does two things:

  • raises polarity → moderate oil/fuel resistance (between non-polar EPDM and polar NBR);
  • being electron-withdrawing, it deactivates the C=C toward ozone (ozone is electrophilic) → good weather/ozone resistance despite an unsaturated backbone; Cl also makes it flame-retardant.
Correction: neoprene is not “very soluble in oil” — it has FAIR/moderate oil resistance. Heavy swelling usually means an aromatic or chlorinated fluid, not straight mineral oil.

This guidance follows the selection philosophy of industry non-metallic materials specifications (e.g. an industry non-metallic materials DEP specification (DEP 30.10.02.13-class)) and the governing qualification standards ISO 23936-1 (thermoplastics), ISO 23936-2 (elastomers) and EN 13121 (GRP). It is engineering guidance, not a substitute for project piping classes or vendor qualification.

1 · Materials selection process

The material shall be compatible with the service fluids over the full design temperature range so that the mechanical, physical and chemical properties satisfy the design requirements throughout the intended lifetime. Piping classes define the design envelope (temperature, pressure, concentration) for each service.

Service fluid & concentration

The dominant selection driver. Identify all wetted media (hydrocarbons, water/steam, acids, amines, methanol, brines) and their concentrations. Aromatics (BTX) content strongly governs elastomer swell — even <1% can exceed swell limits.

Design temperature envelope

Both minimum and maximum, including upsets, CIP and regeneration peaks. For thermosets, design temperature shall not exceed Tg − 30 °C; composites should not be used below −40 °C.

Pressure & rapid gas decompression (RGD)

For sweet and sour gas above 40 bar (Class 300), resistance to ageing and RGD shall be demonstrated per ISO 23936-2. Acceptance: RGD damage rating < 4 (0 for critical service).

Mechanical duty & creep

Thermoplastic and thermoset lifetime is creep- (stress- and temperature-) limited. High pressure requires modulus/hardness to resist extrusion; dynamic seals require low compression set and wear resistance.

Ageing & swelling qualification

Long-term property change under fluid exposure at temperature is qualified by ageing and swell testing per ISO 23936-2. The design shall account for swell and the associated loss of strength/stiffness.

Cost & availability shortlist

FFKM resists nearly everything but is expensive — specify only where lower-cost families cannot meet the duty. Confirm the final grade against the manufacturer datasheet and project piping class.

3 · Family engineering notes

Thermoplastics (qualify to ISO 23936-1)

  • PVC-U / PVC-C — water & industrial service; design temp limited (PVC-U ≤ 60 °C). Chemical resistance per ISO 10358 / ISO 4433-3. Strength is creep-limited with temperature.
  • PE-HD — liner/pipe for oilfield; max design temp 100 °C; H₂S limited to 3 mol%; aromatics may cause excessive swell.
  • Polyamide (PA-11/PA-12) — seals, liners, flexible-pipe sheaths; lifetime limited by creep and ageing (temperature, pH, methanol) — assess per API TR 17TR2.
  • Fluoropolymers (PTFE, ECTFE, PFA, FEP, PVDF) — near-universal chemical resistance; shall not be used with fluorine gas, alkali/reducing metals (Na, K). PVDF standalone piping for high-concentration HCl up to 60 °C.
  • PPS & PEEK — seals, back-ups and supports; high temperature and broad chemical resistance; qualify per ISO 23936-1. Load-carrying limits are creep- and stress-dependent.

Thermosets & composites (GRE / GRVE / GRUP)

  • Max allowable design temperature is set by resin type and cure: design temp ≤ Tg − 30 °C. Typical maxima: GRE 85–100 °C, GRVE 90–100 °C, GRUP 60–90 °C.
  • Do not use below −40 °C (freezing damage risk in water service). Chemical resistance of resins per EN 13121; liner systems extend service (e.g. PVC-U lined GRVE for HCl/NaOCl).
  • GRE pipelines: suitable for water/oil/wet gas/multiphase; H₂S ≤ 7 mol%; not for dry gas; acid service requires an acid-resistant liner.

Elastomers (qualify to ISO 23936-2)

  • Qualification covers ageing, RGD and mechanical/physical testing. For gas > 40 bar, RGD resistance shall be demonstrated (damage rating < 4; 0 for critical).
  • Upstream temperature limits (indicative, per DEP Table 9): NBR ≈ −40…115 °C, HNBR ≈ −40…160 °C (compounded up to 180 °C in sour), FKM ≈ −15…204 °C, FFKM ≈ −15…315 °C, FEPM sour to 205 °C.
  • Nitriles swell more than fluoroelastomers in hydrocarbons and are unsuitable with aromatics. FKM types 1/3 are attacked by neat methanol (type 2 resists).
  • Downstream — amine service: FFKM > FEPM > EPDM. FKM is not suitable. Peroxide-cured EPDM (<200 ppm halogen) may be used up to 120 °C; special amine-resistant FFKM up to 150 °C.
  • Downstream — HCl: EPDM gaskets up to 20% HCl; ePTFE above 20% (requires ~10 MPa seating stress).

Metallic (complementary)

  • Metals cover duties beyond polymer limits: high temperature/pressure, structural load, and aggressive media where elastomers/plastics fail. Common in gaskets (spiral-wound SS316 + graphite/PTFE filler), seat/trim hardfacing, bolting and springs.
  • Sour service (H₂S) selection shall comply with NACE MR0175 / ISO 15156. Austenitic SS (304/316) is prone to chloride stress-corrosion cracking above ~60 °C — prefer duplex, super duplex, Ni alloys or titanium for seawater/chlorides.

3.6 · Illustrated acceptance criteria

Three concepts govern elastomer qualification and are easiest to grasp visually: swelling, rapid gas decompression (RGD), and the RGD damage rating.

Swelling — fluid absorbed into the network

Driven by polarity match (“like dissolves like”). The fluid diffuses between chains and the seal grows in volume, losing strength/stiffness. Design must allow for swell.

Rapid Gas Decompression (RGD / explosive decompression)

A PHYSICAL failure, not a chemical one. Under high pressure, gas dissolves into the elastomer; on fast depressurisation it cannot escape in time, nucleates bubbles and tears the material internally. Worse with small soluble gases (CO₂, H₂S), high pressure, fast decompression, low modulus.

RGD damage rating (ISO 23936-2)

After testing, the seal is sectioned and rated by number/length of internal cracks. Acceptance: rating < 4; rating 0 (no visible damage) for critical service.

Seal selection in practice — bases, property limits, failure modes

Practical layer on top of the chemistry: how the industry actually screens a seal compound. Sources: ASTM / ISO / NORSOK / ISO 23936, consolidated with vendor guidance.

A · The selection dimensions

  • Medium / fluid compatibility — resist every contacted fluid (process oil/gas/water, air, assembly lubricants, cleaning agents). “Like dissolves like”: a chemical structure DISSIMILAR to the fluid avoids swell/degradation.
  • Temperature range — continuous + start-up/shut-down extremes + thermal cycling. Heat accelerates chemical attack & hardening/flow; cold causes “freezing”/brittleness.
  • Pressure — constant vs pulsating sets the hardness/modulus needed to resist extrusion & “nibbling” into clearance gaps.
  • Application type — static vs dynamic (rotary/reciprocating). Dynamic needs abrasion resistance, low friction, good resilience.
  • Hardware & surface finish — performance is tied to the mating metal’s roughness (Ra) and hardness.

Metallic selection rests on one idea: a thin, self-healing passive oxide film (mainly Cr₂O₃) protects the alloy. Alloying elements stabilise that film; the service environment tries to break it (chlorides, reducing acids, high temperature). Match the alloy to the attack.

Chapter 2 · Alloy family tree — the selection logic

Read left→right = add an element, gain a property. This is the backbone of metallic selection: start from a base grade and move along the spine your environment demands (chloride/pitting, strength, or severe chemical).

Carbon & low-alloy steel (from plain carbon steel) — +alloy → strength / high-T / sour / cryogenic →

  • Plain carbon steel Fe-C base
    • Mild / low-C — A106, A53, A516 pipe, plate, structural (sweet + inhibitor)
    • Medium-C — A105 +C → forgings, flanges / fittings
    • Cr-Mo low-alloy — F11 (1.25Cr), F22 (2.25Cr-1Mo), P91 (9Cr) +Cr +Mo → high-T / hydrogen service
    • Q&T high-strength — 4130, 4140, 4340 quench & temper → wellhead / subsea (sour ≤22 HRC, MR0175)
    • HIC/SSC-resistant — low-S, Ca-treated sour wet service (NACE)
    • Cryogenic / Ni low-alloy Group B
      • Carbon-Manganese — A537 0.2C-1.5Mn · +Mn → toughness, LNG/LPG shells (Group B-0)
      • 1.5–3.5% Ni steel — A203 0.1C-1.5/3.5Ni · +Ni → toughness to -100°C (Group B-1)
        • 9% Ni steel — A353 / A553 0.1C-9Ni · +Ni → toughness to -196°C, LNG (Group B-2)

Austenitic (from 304) — increasing corrosion resistance →

  • 304 base · 18Cr-8Ni
    • 304L low C → weld / anti-sensitisation

      • 316L low C → welds
      • 317 more Mo

      • 316Ti Ti-stabilised 316 → Mo + weld stability
      • 347 / 347H Nb-stabilised → welds, high-T

Duplex — increasing Cr/Mo/N → chloride + strength →

  • 2304 lean duplex · economy + strength

Ferritic (from 430)

  • 430 base · 16–18Cr

    • 439 / 444 +Ti/Nb +Mo → corrosion + weldable

Martensitic + PH (from 410) — hardness after HT →

  • 410 base · 12Cr · quench & temper
    • 416 +S → free-machining
    • 420 +C → higher hardness
      • 440A/B/C ↑C → max hardness (bearings)
    • 431 +Cr +Ni → corrosion + toughness
    • Super 13Cr +Ni +Mo → CO₂ tubulars
    • 17-4 / 15-5 / 13-8 PH age-hardened → high strength

Nickel-base (γ matrix) — when Fe-Cr is not enough →

  • Nickel fcc γ matrix
    • Ni-Cu → Monel 400 / K-500 HF, seawater, reducing (K-500 aged)
    • Ni 200/201 hot caustic (NaOH)
    • Ni-Cr → Inconel 600 / 690 oxidising acids, high-T
    • Ni-Fe-Cr-Mo → 825 / 20Cb-3 sulphuric/phosphoric acid, sour
    • Ni-Cr-Mo-Nb → 625 / 718 broad + high strength (718 aged) · PREN ~51

    • Ni-Mo → Hastelloy B-2 / B-3 hot HCl (reducing)

Titanium (from CP Ti) — +Pd / alloy → crevice / strength →

  • CP Titanium α, unalloyed
    • Gr 1 lowest strength, most formable
    • Gr 2 workhorse → seawater, oxidising chlorides
      • Gr 3 / Gr 4 +O/Fe → higher strength CP
      • Gr 7 / Gr 11 +Pd → crevice / reducing-acid

    • CuNi 90/10 (C70600) seawater piping, antifouling

    • Ni-Al bronze (C63000) erosion — pumps / valves

Chapter 5 · Selection rules

  • Chloride SCC: austenitic SS (304/316) crack under tensile stress + chlorides above ~60°C → prefer duplex / super-duplex, Ni alloys or titanium for seawater.
  • Sour (H₂S) service: selection shall comply with NACE MR0175 / ISO 15156 (hardness limits, qualified CRAs).
  • Oxidizing vs reducing: oxidizing acids (HNO₃) favour high-Cr, film-forming alloys; reducing acids (HCl, H₂SO₄, deaerated) favour high-Mo/Ni (Hastelloy C, Alloy 625, Ni, Ti).
  • Intergranular / welds: use L-grades (low C) or Ti/Nb-stabilised alloys to avoid sensitisation.
  • Galvanic: avoid large potential difference between coupled metals in an electrolyte — the less-noble metal corrodes faster.
